Dear Editors,

Please consider the manuscript “Multi-Scale Persistent Homology for Competitive Collective Systems” as a research article in the *Journal of Applied and Computational Topology*.

Competitive collective systems are sets of agents in a bounded domain that coordinate internally while responding to an opponent. They organise at several named levels at once: individuals, small groups, and a system-wide envelope. Persistent homology records connected components and loops as a distance parameter grows. On the full point cloud those levels occupy one diagram, so a change in the diagram cannot be assigned to a level.

The manuscript makes the homology scale-attributable by changing the input. Levels are named by how many groups they contain, not by a length assumed in advance. A cutoff sweep reads those distances from the group-count curve. Hierarchical clustering at the recovered lengths replaces each group by its centroid, and the Vietoris–Rips filtration is computed on that reduced cloud, out to its diameter. The algebraic steps are standard. The contribution is that the composition gives one diagram per named level on a real, high-frequency, noisy point cloud, and that, on the primary match, every finite loop has a simple-cycle representative, present at birth by construction.

Professional football is the measurement setting, because the levels are named and the positions are recorded in metres. On ten broadcast-tracked matches, with all 22 players at 10 Hz, the named group-count targets are met at 2.75 m, 11.75 m, and 23.0 m. At the team cutoff, four centroids occur in 141 of 436,648 frames (a loop needs four vertices); none of those frames carries a loop. Loops appear at the individual and tactical levels. The two loop-bearing levels are associated at Spearman ρ = 0.234. Applying the pipeline to another bounded competitive system requires re-deriving the interaction lengths, which is not tested here.

This is the bridge the journal exists to publish: an essential use of computational topology on a scientific measurement problem, written for both topologists and users of topological methods. The question is how to attribute persistent features to an organisational scale. The tracking data are the test of that question.

The manuscript is original and is not under consideration by another journal. A preprint has been submitted to arXiv and is being processed. I am the sole author and the corresponding author, and I approve this submission.
