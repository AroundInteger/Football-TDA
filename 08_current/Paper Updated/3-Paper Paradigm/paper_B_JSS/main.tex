\begin{abstract}
Topological data analysis can detect ring-like gaps in player positioning, including pressing traps and breaks between defensive lines, that standard geometric
descriptors of team shape do not directly capture. Using a multi-scale
persistent homology method validated in a companion paper, this study
tested what these topological measures reveal about football across ten
professional matches, beyond confirming the method itself is sound.
Topological persistence tracked match events coherently: events that
disrupt team shape, including on-ball engagements, quick breaks, and defensive challenges, were followed by a fall in persistence, while sustained
build-up play was followed by a rise, a pattern that held across multiple
event-window widths. Tactical-scale persistence correlated only weakly
with four geometric descriptors already used in football analytics, team length, team width, convex-hull area, and Voronoi dispersion entropy, and explained variation in team shape that those descriptors did not,
indicating it is not redundant with existing measures. Home and away
teams' tactical structures evolved largely independently of one another
within a match. That non-redundancy did not, however, translate into a
detectable improvement in predicting phase of play on held-out matches at
the present sample size. Persistence appears to provide an automatically
computable, non-redundant measure of tactical state, with clear practical
value for monitoring team shape at scale; whether it can improve
predictive models will require testing on a substantially larger sample,
which a companion full-season analysis is addressing.
\end{abstract}
